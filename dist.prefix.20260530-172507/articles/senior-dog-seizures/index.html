Senior Dog Seizures: Causes, Diagnosis, and Emergency Care

By Amelia Keller · Senior Editor

Published May 28, 2026

Seizures in senior dogs can be a terrifying experience for both pet and owner. Witnessing your beloved companion convulse, lose consciousness, or exhibit unusual behavior is understandably distressing. However, understanding the potential causes, knowing what to do during a seizure, and recognizing when to seek immediate veterinary care can make a significant difference in your dog’s well-being. This article provides a practical guide to navigating senior dog seizures, focusing on actionable steps you can take.

Understanding Seizures in Senior Dogs

A seizure is a sudden surge of electrical activity in the brain, causing a temporary disturbance in normal brain function. In senior dogs, seizures are often symptomatic, meaning they are caused by an underlying medical condition. While seizures can occur at any age, certain causes are more prevalent in older dogs.

Common Causes of Seizures in Senior Dogs

Identifying the underlying cause of seizures is crucial for effective management. Some of the most common causes in senior dogs include:

  • Brain Tumors: As dogs age, the risk of developing brain tumors increases. These tumors can disrupt normal brain function and trigger seizures.
  • Idiopathic Epilepsy: While more commonly diagnosed in younger dogs, some cases of idiopathic epilepsy (epilepsy with no identifiable cause) may not manifest until later in life. Though less common in seniors, it is still considered.
  • Stroke or Cerebrovascular Disease: Strokes, caused by a disruption of blood flow to the brain, can damage brain tissue and lead to seizures.
  • Metabolic Disorders: Conditions such as liver disease, kidney failure, and electrolyte imbalances can affect brain function and trigger seizures.
  • Cognitive Dysfunction Syndrome (CDS): In some cases, seizures can be a symptom of CDS, a neurodegenerative condition similar to Alzheimer’s disease in humans. Learn more about the early signs of dog cognitive dysfunction.
  • Toxicity: Exposure to certain toxins, such as lead, pesticides, or even some medications, can induce seizures.
  • Infections: Brain infections, such as encephalitis, can also cause seizures in senior dogs.

Types of Seizures in Dogs

Seizures can manifest in various ways, and recognizing the different types is important for providing accurate information to your veterinarian.

  • Generalized Seizures: These seizures involve the entire brain and typically result in a loss of consciousness, convulsions (uncontrolled muscle movements), paddling of the limbs, salivation, urination, and defecation.
  • Focal Seizures: Also known as partial seizures, these seizures affect only a specific area of the brain. The symptoms can vary depending on the affected region, but may include facial twitching, limb jerking, behavioral changes, or sensory disturbances. Focal seizures can sometimes progress to generalized seizures.
  • Cluster Seizures: This refers to multiple seizures occurring within a short period (e.g., 24 hours). Cluster seizures are considered a medical emergency, as they can lead to status epilepticus.
  • Status Epilepticus: This is a prolonged seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes, or multiple seizures occurring without the dog regaining consciousness in between. Status epilepticus is a life-threatening condition that requires immediate veterinary intervention.

What to Do During a Senior Dog Seizure

Witnessing your dog having a seizure can be frightening, but staying calm and taking the following steps can help ensure their safety:

  1. Stay Calm: Your dog needs you to be calm and in control. Take a deep breath and focus on the steps you need to take.
  2. Protect Your Dog: Gently move any objects that could harm your dog during the seizure. If possible, cushion their head with a soft blanket or pillow.
  3. Do Not Restrain Your Dog: It’s important not to restrain your dog during a seizure. This can cause injury to both you and your dog.
  4. Time the Seizure: Note the start and end time of the seizure. This information is crucial for your veterinarian. Seizures lasting longer than 5 minutes require immediate veterinary attention.
  5. Observe Your Dog: Pay attention to the type of seizure, the symptoms your dog is exhibiting, and any changes in their behavior before, during, and after the seizure.
  6. Keep Your Distance From the Mouth: Dogs do not swallow their tongues during a seizure. Keep your hands away from their mouth to avoid being bitten.
  7. Provide a Safe and Quiet Space After the Seizure: After the seizure, your dog will likely be disoriented and confused. Provide them with a quiet and comfortable space to recover. Stay with them and speak to them in a calm and reassuring voice.

When to Seek Emergency Veterinary Care

Certain situations warrant immediate veterinary attention during or after a seizure. Seek emergency care if:

  • The seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes (status epilepticus).
  • Your dog has cluster seizures (multiple seizures within a short period).
  • Your dog has difficulty breathing after the seizure.
  • Your dog injures themselves during the seizure.
  • Your dog does not regain consciousness after the seizure.
  • This is your dog’s first seizure.
  • Your dog has a known history of seizures, but the seizure is different or more severe than usual.

Diagnosis and Management of Seizures

Diagnosing the underlying cause of seizures in senior dogs typically involves a thorough veterinary examination, including:

  • Neurological Exam: To assess brain function and identify any neurological deficits.
  • Blood Tests: To evaluate organ function and rule out metabolic disorders.
  • Urine Analysis: To assess kidney function and detect any infections.
  • Imaging Studies: MRI or CT scans of the brain may be necessary to identify brain tumors, strokes, or other structural abnormalities.
  • Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Analysis: In some cases, a CSF tap may be performed to rule out infections or inflammatory conditions of the brain.

Treatment Options

Treatment for seizures depends on the underlying cause. If a specific cause is identified, such as a brain tumor or metabolic disorder, treatment will focus on addressing that condition. However, in many cases, the cause of seizures remains unknown (idiopathic epilepsy). In such cases, anticonvulsant medications may be prescribed to help control the seizures. Common anticonvulsant medications for dogs include phenobarbital, potassium bromide, and levetiracetam (Keppra).

It is important to note that anticonvulsant medications may not completely eliminate seizures, but they can often reduce their frequency and severity. Regular monitoring by your veterinarian is necessary to adjust the medication dosage and monitor for any side effects.

Dietary Considerations

While diet alone cannot cure seizures, certain dietary adjustments may help manage the condition in some dogs.

  • High-Quality Senior Diet: Feeding a high-quality, balanced senior diet tailored to your dog’s specific needs is essential for overall health and well-being.   • Omega-3 Fatty Acids: Supplementing with omega-3 fatty acids may have neuroprotective effects and potentially reduce seizure frequency.
  • Avoidance of Certain Additives: Some pet owners believe that certain food additives or preservatives may trigger seizures in susceptible dogs. While scientific evidence is limited, it may be worth considering a diet free of artificial colors, flavors, and preservatives.

Seizures in senior dogs can be a challenging condition to manage, but with a thorough understanding of the causes, proper emergency care, and appropriate veterinary treatment, you can help improve your dog’s quality of life and provide them with the best possible care in their golden years. Remember to work closely with your veterinarian to develop an individualized management plan for your dog’s specific needs.
