Senior Dog Sleeping More Than Usual: When Is It a Problem?

By Amelia Keller · Senior Editor

Published May 28, 2026

As your dog enters their golden years, you might notice they’re spending more time snoozing. While increased sleep is a natural part of aging, it’s important to know when this change is simply a sign of slowing down and when it could indicate an underlying health issue. After all, our senior dogs can’t tell us what’s wrong, and “Dr. Google” often leads to unnecessary worry. Here’s a comprehensive guide to help you understand your senior dog’s sleep patterns, differentiate normal aging from potential problems, and know when a vet visit is necessary.

Normal Sleep Changes in Senior Dogs

Just like humans, dogs experience changes in their sleep patterns as they age. These changes are often gradual and can be attributed to a variety of factors related to the aging process itself.

Why Senior Dogs Sleep More

  • Slower Metabolism: As dogs age, their metabolism slows down, leading to decreased energy levels. This can result in increased periods of rest and sleep.
  • Reduced Activity Levels: Senior dogs often become less active due to arthritis, decreased muscle mass, or general fatigue. Less activity naturally leads to more downtime and sleep. A dog who used to love a daily two-mile walk might now be content with a gentle stroll around the block.
  • Changes in Circadian Rhythm: The circadian rhythm, the body’s internal clock, can become less stable with age. This can result in less consistent sleep patterns, including longer periods of sleep and more frequent naps throughout the day.
  • Decreased Sensory Perception: Older dogs may experience a decline in their senses, such as hearing and vision. This can make them feel less engaged with their environment, leading to increased sleep. They might also sleep more out of boredom if their ability to interact with their surroundings is diminished.

What’s Considered “Too Much” Sleep?

There’s no magic number for how much a senior dog should sleep. However, a general guideline is that senior dogs often sleep 14-16 hours a day, compared to the 12-14 hours younger adult dogs typically sleep. The key is to look for changes in your dog’s usual behavior. If your dog suddenly starts sleeping significantly more than usual, or if their sleep patterns are accompanied by other concerning symptoms, it’s time to investigate further.

Red flags to watch for include:

  • Sudden increase in sleep duration (e.g., sleeping almost all day).
  • Lethargy or reluctance to engage in activities they once enjoyed.
  • Changes in appetite or water intake.
  • Difficulty getting up or moving around.
  • Behavioral changes, such as increased irritability or confusion.

Potential Health Problems Linked to Increased Sleep

While increased sleep can be a normal part of aging, it can also be a symptom of underlying health problems that require veterinary attention. Recognizing these potential issues early can improve your dog’s quality of life and potentially extend their lifespan.

Common Culprits

  • Arthritis and Joint Pain: Arthritis is a common condition in senior dogs. The pain and discomfort associated with arthritis can make it difficult for dogs to move around, leading to increased periods of rest and sleep. If your dog is sleeping more and also seems stiff, limps, or has difficulty getting up or down, arthritis is a likely suspect. Consider a high-quality joint supplement containing glucosamine and chondroitin, like Nutramax Cosequin for Dogs. We’ve found it particularly effective when paired with consistent, gentle exercise.
  • Hypothyroidism: Hypothyroidism, or an underactive thyroid gland, is another common condition in older dogs. It can cause a variety of symptoms, including lethargy, weight gain, hair loss, and increased sleep. If your dog is sleeping more and also exhibiting these other symptoms, a blood test can help determine if hypothyroidism is the cause.
  • Diabetes: Diabetes can also lead to increased sleepiness in dogs. Other signs of diabetes include increased thirst, increased urination, and weight loss despite a normal or increased appetite.
  • Kidney Disease: As kidney function declines, toxins can build up in the bloodstream, leading to lethargy and increased sleep. Other symptoms of kidney disease include increased thirst, increased urination, decreased appetite, and vomiting.
  • Heart Disease: Heart problems can reduce blood flow, leading to fatigue and weakness. This can cause your dog to sleep more. Other signs of heart disease include coughing, difficulty breathing, and exercise intolerance.
  • Cognitive Dysfunction Syndrome (CDS): Similar to Alzheimer’s disease in humans, CDS can cause confusion, disorientation, changes in sleep-wake cycles, and decreased interaction with family members. When to See a Vet

If you notice a sudden or significant increase in your senior dog’s sleep, especially if it’s accompanied by any of the other symptoms mentioned above, it’s crucial to schedule a vet appointment. Early diagnosis and treatment can make a significant difference in managing these conditions and improving your dog’s quality of life. Don’t wait for symptoms to worsen; proactive veterinary care is key.

How to Support Your Sleeping Senior Dog

Whether your dog’s increased sleep is due to normal aging or an underlying health condition, there are several things you can do to support their comfort and well-being.

Creating a Comfortable Sleep Environment

  • Provide a Supportive Bed: An orthopedic bed can provide much-needed support for achy joints and muscles. Look for a bed made of memory foam or other high-density materials. Consider a heated bed, especially during colder months. See also: [Heated beds for senior pets: safe options and which to avoid](insert link here).
  • Ensure Easy Access: Make sure your dog’s bed is easily accessible, especially if they have mobility issues. If they struggle to get up on the bed, consider using a ramp or steps. If they tend to wander at night, keep their bed in a safe, enclosed area.
  • Maintain a Consistent Routine: A consistent daily routine can help regulate your dog’s sleep-wake cycle and reduce anxiety. Try to feed them, walk them, and provide playtime at the same times each day.

Diet and Exercise Adjustments

  • Adjust Their Diet: As dogs age, their nutritional needs change. Senior dog foods are often formulated with lower calorie counts and higher levels of fiber to help maintain a healthy weight and support digestive health. If your dog has any underlying health conditions, such as kidney disease or diabetes, your vet may recommend a specific diet. For small breeds, consider Hill’s Science Diet Senior 11+ Small & Mini for its easy-to-chew kibble.
  • Encourage Gentle Exercise: While your senior dog may not be able to handle the same level of activity as they used to, regular gentle exercise is still important for maintaining their physical and mental health. Short walks, swimming, or gentle stretching exercises can help keep their joints mobile and prevent muscle loss.

Monitoring and Documentation

  • Keep a Sleep Diary: Tracking your dog’s sleep patterns can help you identify any significant changes and provide valuable information to your vet. Note the times your dog goes to sleep and wakes up, as well as any unusual behaviors or symptoms you observe.
  • Take Videos: A short video of your dog trying to get up, walk, or even just resting can be incredibly helpful for your vet. It’s often easier to show a vet a quick video than to try and describe subtle changes in gait or behavior.

By understanding the normal sleep changes that occur in senior dogs and being aware of potential health problems that can cause increased sleep, you can provide your furry friend with the best possible care during their golden years. Remember, proactive monitoring, prompt veterinary attention, and a supportive home environment are key to ensuring your senior dog enjoys a comfortable and fulfilling life.
