Senior Dog Diarrhea: Causes, Remedies, and When to See a Vet

By Amelia Keller · Senior Editor

Published May 28, 2026

Diarrhea in senior dogs is, unfortunately, a common occurrence. While a single episode might be a minor blip, frequent or severe bouts of diarrhea warrant a closer look. Unlike younger dogs, older dogs are often dealing with underlying health conditions that can make them more susceptible to digestive upset. This article will walk you through the common causes of senior dog diarrhea, how to manage it at home, and, most importantly, when it’s time to seek professional veterinary help.

Common Causes of Diarrhea in Senior Dogs

Pinpointing the exact cause of your dog’s diarrhea can be tricky, but understanding the possibilities is the first step. Here are some of the most frequent culprits in older dogs:

Dietary Indiscretions

Just like us, senior dogs can get diarrhea from eating something they shouldn’t. This could include scavenging food from the trash, getting into table scraps, or even a sudden change in their regular diet. Remember that “senior” food is often lower calorie than standard adult food, and switching rapidly can cause GI upset. Gradual transitions are key (see our article: [Senior dog food transitions without GI upset](11. Senior dog food transitions without GI upset)).

Infections

Bacterial, viral, or parasitic infections can wreak havoc on a senior dog’s digestive system. Common culprits include:

  • Parasites: Giardia, coccidia, roundworms, hookworms, and whipworms are all intestinal parasites that can cause diarrhea. Your vet can diagnose these with a fecal test.
  • Bacterial Infections: Salmonella and E. coli are just two examples of bacteria that can lead to diarrhea.
  • Viral Infections: While less common in vaccinated senior dogs, parvovirus and coronavirus can still occur, especially in dogs with weakened immune systems.

Medication Side Effects

Many medications commonly prescribed to senior dogs, such as antibiotics, NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for pain), and even some heart medications, can cause diarrhea as a side effect. Always discuss potential side effects with your veterinarian when starting a new medication.

Underlying Health Conditions

Diarrhea can be a symptom of more serious underlying health conditions that are more prevalent in senior dogs:

  • Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD): This chronic inflammatory condition can cause persistent diarrhea and vomiting.
  • Pancreatitis: Inflammation of the pancreas can disrupt digestion and lead to diarrhea.
  • Kidney Disease: As kidney function declines, toxins can build up in the body, leading to gastrointestinal upset.
  • Liver Disease: Similar to kidney disease, liver dysfunction can also cause diarrhea.
  • Cancer: Certain types of cancer, particularly those affecting the digestive tract, can cause diarrhea.
  • Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency (EPI): This condition occurs when the pancreas doesn’t produce enough digestive enzymes.

Stress and Anxiety

Stress and anxiety can also contribute to diarrhea in senior dogs. Changes in routine, loud noises, or separation anxiety can all trigger digestive upset. If your dog is also showing other signs of anxiety, like pacing, panting, or excessive barking, stress might be the culprit.

At-Home Management of Senior Dog Diarrhea

For mild cases of diarrhea, you may be able to manage your dog’s symptoms at home. However, it’s crucial to monitor your dog closely and seek veterinary care if their condition doesn’t improve or worsens.

The Bland Diet Approach

A bland diet is often recommended for dogs with diarrhea. This typically consists of easily digestible foods that are gentle on the digestive system.

  • Boiled Chicken and White Rice: A classic combination. Use skinless, boneless chicken breast and plain white rice. The ratio should be about 2 parts rice to 1 part chicken.
  • Lean Ground Beef and White Rice: Another option, but make sure to drain off any excess fat after cooking the ground beef.
  • Plain Cooked Sweet Potato: Sweet potato is a good source of fiber and can help firm up stools.
  • Avoid: Dairy products, fatty foods, and highly processed treats.

Feed small, frequent meals throughout the day instead of large meals. This can help reduce the burden on the digestive system. For example, if your dog normally eats two cups of food a day, divide that into four half-cup servings.

Hydration is Key

Diarrhea can lead to dehydration, so it’s essential to ensure your dog is drinking enough water.

  • Fresh Water: Always provide fresh, clean water.
  • Electrolyte Solutions: Unflavored Pedialyte or a dog-specific electrolyte solution can help replenish lost electrolytes. Offer it in small amounts, as too much can worsen diarrhea.
  • Bone Broth: Unsalted bone broth can be a palatable way to encourage hydration and provide some nutrients.

If your dog is refusing to drink, you can try offering water or electrolyte solutions via a syringe. Contact your vet right away if your dog shows signs of dehydration, such as dry gums, sunken eyes, or decreased skin elasticity. Probiotics

Probiotics can help restore the balance of beneficial bacteria in the gut. Look for a dog-specific probiotic supplement. Human probiotics may not be as effective for dogs. Follow the dosage instructions on the product label. We’ve seen good results with FortiFlora, but always consult with your vet before adding any supplement to your dog’s diet.

When to See a Veterinarian

While home remedies can be helpful for mild cases of diarrhea, certain signs indicate that it’s time to seek veterinary attention:

  • Blood in the stool: This can indicate a serious underlying problem.
  • Black, tarry stools: This suggests digested blood, which could indicate bleeding in the upper digestive tract.
  • Vomiting: Especially if the vomiting is frequent or contains blood.
  • Lethargy or weakness: This could indicate dehydration or a more serious underlying condition.
  • Loss of appetite: If your dog refuses to eat for more than 24 hours.
  • Abdominal pain: If your dog is showing signs of discomfort in their abdomen, such as guarding their belly or whining when touched.
  • Fever: A fever can indicate an infection.
  • Diarrhea lasting more than 48 hours: If the diarrhea doesn’t improve after a couple of days of home treatment.
  • Pre-existing health conditions: If your dog has any underlying health conditions, such as diabetes, kidney disease, or heart disease, it’s always best to consult with your veterinarian.

What to Expect at the Vet

Your veterinarian will perform a physical exam and ask you questions about your dog’s symptoms, diet, medications, and medical history. They may also recommend diagnostic tests, such as:

  • Fecal exam: To check for parasites.
  • Blood tests: To assess organ function and look for signs of infection or inflammation.
  • Urinalysis: To evaluate kidney function.
  • X-rays or ultrasound: To visualize the abdominal organs and look for abnormalities.
  • Endoscopy or colonoscopy: In some cases, your veterinarian may recommend these procedures to examine the digestive tract more closely.

Treatment will depend on the underlying cause of the diarrhea. This may include:

  • Medications: Antibiotics, antiparasitics, anti-inflammatories, or other medications to treat the underlying cause.
  • Fluid therapy: To rehydrate your dog if they are dehydrated.

  • Supportive care: Probiotics, anti-nausea medications, or other medications to help manage symptoms.

Diarrhea in senior dogs can be a sign of a wide range of health issues. By understanding the potential causes and knowing when to seek veterinary care, you can help ensure your senior dog gets the treatment they need to stay healthy and comfortable.
