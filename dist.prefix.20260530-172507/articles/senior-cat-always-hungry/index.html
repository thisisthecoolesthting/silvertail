Senior Cat Always Hungry: Understanding Increased Appetite and What to Do

It’s unsettling when your senior cat suddenly seems insatiable. One minute they’re content with their usual meals, the next they’re acting like they haven’t eaten in days, constantly meowing for food, and even getting into things they shouldn’t. While it might seem like a simple case of increased hunger, it can be a sign of underlying health issues that require attention. Understanding the potential causes of a senior cat’s increased appetite is the first step in ensuring they receive the proper care.

This article will explore the common reasons behind increased appetite in older cats, helping you differentiate between normal age-related changes and potential medical concerns. We’ll also provide guidance on how to manage your cat’s diet and when it’s crucial to consult with your veterinarian.

Is It Just Old Age?

While a sudden, dramatic increase in appetite should always be investigated, it’s important to acknowledge that some changes are simply part of the aging process. Senior cats (generally those over 11 years old) may experience changes in their metabolism and nutrient absorption. They might not process food as efficiently as they used to, leading to a genuine feeling of hunger even after eating a normal portion. This is especially true if they are less active than they once were; they still need energy but aren’t burning as many calories.

However, it’s crucial to rule out any underlying medical conditions before attributing the increased appetite solely to old age.

Common Medical Causes of Increased Appetite in Senior Cats

Several medical conditions can lead to increased appetite in senior cats. These conditions often affect the cat’s metabolism, ability to absorb nutrients, or sense of satiety.

Hyperthyroidism

Hyperthyroidism is one of the most common causes of increased appetite in older cats. This condition occurs when the thyroid gland produces excessive thyroid hormones, leading to a rapid metabolism. Cats with hyperthyroidism often exhibit a ravenous appetite, yet paradoxically, they may also lose weight. Other symptoms can include increased thirst, hyperactivity, vomiting, and diarrhea.

What to do: If you suspect your cat has hyperthyroidism, a visit to the veterinarian is essential. A simple blood test can confirm the diagnosis. Treatment options include medication, radioactive iodine therapy, and surgery. Medication is typically the first line of defense, but radioactive iodine is often considered the gold standard as it can be curative in many cases.

Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes, specifically diabetes mellitus, can also cause increased hunger in senior cats. In this condition, the cat’s body either doesn’t produce enough insulin or can’t effectively use the insulin it produces. Insulin is crucial for regulating blood sugar levels. When insulin is deficient or ineffective, glucose can’t enter the cells for energy, leading to increased blood sugar and a feeling of starvation. Diabetic cats may exhibit increased appetite, thirst, and urination. Weight loss is also common, despite the increased food intake.

What to do: Similar to hyperthyroidism, diabetes requires veterinary diagnosis and management. Blood and urine tests are necessary to confirm the diagnosis. Treatment typically involves insulin injections, dietary changes, and regular monitoring of blood glucose levels. A prescription diet formulated for diabetic cats is often recommended.

Intestinal Malabsorption

Certain conditions affecting the digestive system can impair nutrient absorption, leading to increased appetite as the cat tries to compensate for the lack of nutrients. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI) are examples of such conditions.

  • IBD: This involves chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, interfering with nutrient absorption.
  • EPI: This occurs when the pancreas doesn’t produce enough digestive enzymes, hindering the breakdown and absorption of food.

What to do: Suspect malabsorption if your cat has increased appetite, weight loss, diarrhea, and/or vomiting. A vet can run specialized tests to diagnose these conditions, which often require prescription diets or enzyme supplementation.

Other Potential Causes

While hyperthyroidism, diabetes, and malabsorption are the most common culprits, other factors can contribute to increased appetite in senior cats:

  • Parasites: Intestinal parasites can steal nutrients from the cat, leading to increased hunger. Regular deworming is crucial, especially for cats who go outdoors.
  • Medications: Certain medications, such as corticosteroids, can increase appetite as a side effect.
  • Cognitive Dysfunction Syndrome (CDS): While more often associated with decreased appetite or finicky eating, CDS can sometimes manifest as confusion around feeding times or an altered sense of satiety, leading to increased food seeking. Note that cognitive decline can also cause cats to forget they’ve eaten and immediately ask for food again.

Managing Your Senior Cat’s Increased Appetite

Once you’ve consulted with your veterinarian and ruled out any underlying medical conditions, or while you’re awaiting test results, there are several steps you can take to manage your senior cat’s increased appetite:

1. Portion Control and Scheduled Feedings

Avoid free-feeding, where food is available at all times. Instead, divide your cat’s daily food allowance into multiple small meals throughout the day. This can help them feel fuller for longer. Measure the food carefully to avoid overfeeding. A kitchen scale is invaluable for accurate portioning.

2. High-Protein, High-Fiber Diet

Consider switching to a high-protein, high-fiber diet formulated for senior cats. Protein helps maintain muscle mass, while fiber promotes satiety and aids digestion. Look for foods with named meat sources (e.g., chicken, turkey, fish) as the primary ingredient. Avoid foods with excessive fillers or artificial additives. 3. Wet Food Supplementation

Adding wet food to your cat’s diet can increase their water intake and provide a greater sense of fullness due to its higher moisture content. Choose a wet food that complements their dry food in terms of nutritional balance. You can mix a small amount of wet food with their dry food or offer it as a separate meal.

4. Puzzle Feeders and Slow Feeders

Use puzzle feeders or slow feeders to make mealtime more engaging and to slow down their eating. These devices require the cat to work for their food, providing mental stimulation and preventing them from gulping down their meals too quickly.

5. Rule Out Behavioral Issues

Sometimes, increased “hunger” isn’t actually about needing more calories. Boredom, anxiety, or attention-seeking behavior can manifest as increased food solicitation. Ensure your cat has plenty of enrichment opportunities, such as toys, scratching posts, and interactive play sessions. Consider adding a low entry cat tree if your cat still enjoys climbing. If anxiety is suspected, talk to your vet about calming supplements or medication.

6. Monitor Weight and Body Condition

Regularly weigh your cat and assess their body condition. You should be able to feel their ribs without excessive fat covering, but they shouldn’t be prominently visible. A healthy weight is crucial for overall health and can help prevent secondary issues like diabetes or arthritis.

When to See a Veterinarian

While dietary adjustments and enrichment can help manage increased appetite, it’s crucial to seek veterinary attention if you observe any of the following:

  • Sudden and significant increase in appetite
  • Weight loss despite increased food intake
  • Increased thirst and urination
  • Vomiting or diarrhea
  • Lethargy or weakness
  • Changes in behavior or activity level

These symptoms could indicate an underlying medical condition that requires prompt diagnosis and treatment. Don’t hesitate to schedule an appointment with your veterinarian to discuss your concerns and ensure your senior cat receives the best possible care. Remember that early detection and intervention can significantly improve their quality of life.
