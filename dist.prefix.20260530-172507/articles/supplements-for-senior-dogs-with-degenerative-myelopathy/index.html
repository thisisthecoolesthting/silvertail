Supplements for Senior Dogs with Degenerative Myelopathy: Can They Help?

Degenerative myelopathy (DM) is a progressive, incurable disease affecting the spinal cord of older dogs. It often starts with hind limb weakness and incoordination, eventually leading to paralysis. Understandably, owners of dogs diagnosed with DM are eager to find anything that might slow the progression or improve their dog’s quality of life. This often leads to exploring supplements, but are they truly effective, and which ones are worth considering?

Understanding Degenerative Myelopathy

Before diving into supplements, it’s crucial to understand what DM is and what it isn’t. DM is a genetic disease, primarily affecting breeds like German Shepherds, Boxers, and Pembroke Welsh Corgis, although it can occur in other breeds as well. The disease causes degeneration of the white matter in the spinal cord, disrupting the communication between the brain and the limbs.

Key characteristics of DM:

  • Progressive: The condition worsens over time.
  • Painless: DM itself isn’t typically painful, although secondary issues like muscle atrophy or pressure sores can cause discomfort.
  • No Cure: Currently, there is no cure for DM. Management focuses on slowing the progression and maintaining quality of life.
  • Diagnosis: Diagnosis usually involves ruling out other conditions like intervertebral disc disease or tumors through imaging (MRI, CT scan) and potentially genetic testing.

Common Supplements Marketed for DM and the Evidence

Many supplements are marketed for senior dogs with mobility issues, but the evidence supporting their use in specifically slowing or reversing the effects of DM is limited. Here’s a breakdown of some common supplements and the existing research:

1. Antioxidants (Vitamin E, Vitamin C, Selenium)

The Claim: Antioxidants help protect cells from damage caused by free radicals, which are thought to contribute to the progression of DM.

The Evidence: Some studies suggest that oxidative stress plays a role in neurodegenerative diseases. Antioxidants may have a protective effect. However, the evidence specifically for DM in dogs is weak.

Considerations: While generally safe, excessive amounts of some antioxidants can be harmful. For example, high doses of Vitamin C can cause diarrhea. It’s best to discuss antioxidant supplementation with your veterinarian.

2. B Vitamins (B12, B Complex)

The Claim: B vitamins support nerve health and function. B12 deficiency can cause neurological problems, so supplementation might be beneficial.

The Evidence: B vitamins are essential for nerve function, and deficiencies can lead to neurological signs. However, DM isn’t primarily caused by B vitamin deficiency. Some anecdotal reports suggest that B12 injections may provide a temporary boost in energy and appetite, but there’s no solid evidence that they slow DM progression.

Considerations: B vitamins are generally safe, as they are water-soluble and excess amounts are excreted in urine.

3. Omega-3 Fatty Acids (EPA and DHA from Fish Oil)

The Claim: Omega-3 fatty acids have anti-inflammatory properties and may help reduce inflammation in the spinal cord.

The Evidence: Omega-3 fatty acids are known for their anti-inflammatory effects and are commonly used for arthritis management in dogs (see our guide to The Best Joint Supplements for Senior Dogs: What the Research Actually Shows). While DM isn’t primarily an inflammatory disease, some research suggests that inflammation contributes to its progression. However, there’s limited evidence specifically demonstrating that omega-3s slow DM.

Considerations: Choose a high-quality fish oil supplement from a reputable brand. Look for products that have been tested for purity and heavy metals. Start with a low dose and gradually increase it to avoid gastrointestinal upset.

4. Methylsulfonylmethane (MSM)

The Claim: MSM is another anti-inflammatory agent often found in joint supplements.

The Evidence: Similar to Omega-3s, MSM is used more often for arthritis. Its impact on DM is not studied.

Considerations: As with all supplements, quality varies.

5. Other Supplements (SAMe, DMG, etc.)

Many other supplements are marketed for senior dog health, including S-Adenosylmethionine (SAMe) and Dimethylglycine (DMG). These often have only anecdotal support for DM. While they may be helpful for other conditions, their efficacy in slowing DM progression is unproven.

Focusing on What Does Work: Management & Supportive Care

While supplements may offer some marginal benefits, the cornerstone of managing DM is supportive care and interventions that directly address the symptoms and maintain quality of life.

1. Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation

Physical therapy is one of the most effective ways to manage DM. Regular exercise, stretching, and range-of-motion exercises can help maintain muscle mass, improve coordination, and slow the progression of weakness.

  • Hydrotherapy: Swimming or underwater treadmill exercises are excellent for strengthening muscles without putting stress on joints.
  • Massage: Massage can help relieve muscle tension and improve circulation.
  • Assisted Walking: Using a harness or sling to support the hind legs during walks can help your dog maintain mobility and independence for longer.

2. Mobility Aids

As DM progresses, mobility aids become essential.

  • Harnesses and Slings: These can help you support your dog’s hind legs during walks.
  • Dog Wheelchairs: Wheelchairs provide support and allow dogs to continue walking and exercising, even when their hind legs are weak or paralyzed.
  • Non-Slip Flooring: Use rugs or mats to provide better traction and prevent slips and falls (see our guide to Non-slip rug solutions for hardwood floors and older dogs).

3. Preventing Secondary Complications

Dogs with DM are prone to developing secondary complications, such as:

  • Pressure Sores: Due to decreased mobility, pressure sores can develop on bony prominences. Provide soft bedding and regularly change your dog’s position to prevent them.
  • Urinary and Fecal Incontinence: DM can affect bladder and bowel control. Frequent walks and absorbent bedding can help manage incontinence.
  • Muscle Atrophy: Lack of use can lead to muscle atrophy. Physical therapy and regular exercise can help slow this process.

4. Diet and Weight Management

Maintaining a healthy weight is crucial for dogs with DM. Excess weight puts extra stress on the joints and can worsen mobility issues. The Bottom Line

While supplements might play a small role in supporting dogs with degenerative myelopathy, they are not a substitute for comprehensive management and supportive care. Focus on evidence-based strategies like physical therapy, mobility aids, and preventing secondary complications. Always consult with your veterinarian before starting any new supplement regimen. Your vet can help you determine if a particular supplement is appropriate for your dog and can monitor for any potential side effects. The goal is to maintain your dog’s comfort and quality of life for as long as possible, and a multi-faceted approach is the most effective way to achieve that.
