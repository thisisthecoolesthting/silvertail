Senior Dog Coughing at Night: Causes, Remedies, and When to Worry

A persistent cough, especially one that seems to worsen at night, is a common concern for owners of senior dogs. While an occasional cough might be nothing to worry about, a frequent or forceful nighttime cough in an older dog warrants careful observation and, in many cases, a trip to the vet. Nighttime coughing can disrupt your sleep and, more importantly, signal an underlying health issue that needs addressing. This guide will walk you through the potential causes of a senior dog coughing at night, offer practical remedies, and help you determine when it’s time to seek professional veterinary care.

Potential Causes of Nighttime Coughing

Determining the cause of your senior dog’s cough is the first step toward finding effective relief. Here are some of the most common culprits behind nighttime coughing in older dogs:

Heart Disease

Congestive heart failure (CHF) is a frequent cause of coughing in senior dogs. As the heart weakens, it becomes less efficient at pumping blood. This can lead to fluid buildup in the lungs (pulmonary edema), which triggers a cough, often described as a soft, moist cough. Nighttime coughing is particularly common with CHF because dogs are typically lying down, which further increases pressure in the chest and worsens fluid accumulation.

What to look for:

  • Type of cough: Soft, moist, and sometimes accompanied by gagging or retching.
  • Other symptoms: Labored breathing, exercise intolerance, lethargy, decreased appetite, and a bluish tinge to the gums (cyanosis).
  • Typical breeds: Small breeds like Cavalier King Charles Spaniels are predisposed, but any senior dog can develop CHF.

What to do: A vet visit is crucial for diagnosis (usually involving chest X-rays and an echocardiogram) and to initiate appropriate medication to manage the condition. A prescription diet lower in sodium might also be recommended.

Collapsing Trachea

The trachea, or windpipe, is held open by rings of cartilage. In some dogs, these rings weaken with age, causing the trachea to collapse, particularly when the dog inhales. This leads to a harsh, dry cough, often described as a “goose honk.” Nighttime coughing is common, and it can be triggered by excitement, exercise, or pressure on the neck (e.g., from a collar).

What to look for:

  • Type of cough: A harsh, dry “goose honk” sound.
  • Other symptoms: Difficulty breathing, wheezing, gagging, and bluish gums in severe cases. The cough may be triggered by excitement or pulling on the leash.
  • Typical breeds: Small and toy breeds, like Pomeranians, Yorkshire Terriers, and Chihuahuas, are more prone to collapsing trachea.

What to do:

  • Harness instead of collar: Switch to a harness to avoid putting pressure on the trachea.
  • Weight management: If your dog is overweight, work with your vet to develop a weight loss plan.
  • Medications: The vet might prescribe cough suppressants, bronchodilators, or corticosteroids to help manage the symptoms.
  • Humidifier: A humidifier can help to keep the airways moist and reduce irritation.

Kennel Cough (Infectious Tracheobronchitis)

Although often associated with younger dogs, senior dogs can still contract kennel cough, especially if they are in contact with other dogs (e.g., at a boarding facility, dog park, or groomer). Kennel cough is a highly contagious respiratory infection that causes inflammation of the trachea and bronchi.

What to look for:

  • Type of cough: A dry, hacking cough that may be accompanied by gagging or retching. It can sound similar to collapsing trachea, but it’s often more persistent and may be accompanied by other symptoms.
  • Other symptoms: Nasal discharge, sneezing, fever, and lethargy.
  • Risk factors: Recent exposure to other dogs in a communal setting.

What to do:

  • Veterinary visit: See your vet for diagnosis and treatment. They may prescribe antibiotics or cough suppressants.
  • Isolation: Keep your dog isolated from other dogs to prevent the spread of infection.
  • Rest and hydration: Ensure your dog gets plenty of rest and stays hydrated.

Other Respiratory Infections

Besides kennel cough, other viral or bacterial respiratory infections can cause coughing in senior dogs. These infections can irritate the airways and lead to inflammation and mucus production, resulting in a cough.

What to look for:

  • Type of cough: Varies depending on the specific infection, but it’s often a moist cough.
  • Other symptoms: Nasal discharge, sneezing, fever, lethargy, and loss of appetite.

What to do: A vet visit is necessary for diagnosis and treatment. Antibiotics may be prescribed for bacterial infections.

Lung Tumors

Although less common, lung tumors can also cause a cough in senior dogs. The tumor can irritate the airways and lead to fluid buildup in the lungs.

What to look for:

  • Type of cough: Can vary but is often persistent and may worsen over time.
  • Other symptoms: Labored breathing, lethargy, weight loss, and decreased appetite.

What to do: A vet visit is essential for diagnosis, which may involve chest X-rays, CT scans, or biopsies. Treatment options may include surgery, chemotherapy, or radiation therapy, depending on the type and stage of the tumor.

Remedies and Relief for Nighttime Coughing

While addressing the underlying cause is crucial, there are also some steps you can take at home to help alleviate your senior dog’s nighttime coughing:

  • Humidifier: A cool-mist humidifier in the bedroom can help to moisten the air and soothe irritated airways. Clean the humidifier regularly to prevent mold growth.
  • Elevated Bed: Elevating your dog’s head while they sleep can help reduce pressure on the lungs and make breathing easier. Use a wedge-shaped pillow or an orthopedic bed that provides support.
  • Gentle Exercise: Avoid strenuous exercise, especially close to bedtime, as it can exacerbate coughing. Opt for short, gentle walks instead. Consider the Drive Medical Two-Button Folding Walker with WheelsAmazon → if mobility is an issue on those walks.
  • Avoid Irritants: Minimize exposure to smoke, dust, and other airborne irritants that can trigger coughing.
  • Honey: A small amount of honey (1 teaspoon for small dogs, 1 tablespoon for large dogs) can help soothe a cough. However, do not give honey to diabetic dogs.

When to See a Vet

While some home remedies can provide temporary relief, it’s essential to seek veterinary attention if your senior dog’s cough is:

  • Persistent: Lasting for more than a few days.
  • Severe: Accompanied by labored breathing, wheezing, or bluish gums.
  • Worsening: Increasing in frequency or intensity.
  • Accompanied by other symptoms: Such as lethargy, loss of appetite, fever, or nasal discharge.

Don’t hesitate to consult your vet. Early diagnosis and treatment can significantly improve your senior dog’s quality of life and potentially prolong their lifespan. It’s always better to err on the side of caution when it comes to your beloved companion’s health.
