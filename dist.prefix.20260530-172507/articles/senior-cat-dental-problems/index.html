Senior Cat Dental Problems: Prevention, Treatment, and Home Care

By Amelia Keller · Senior Editor

Published May 28, 2026

Dental problems are extremely common in senior cats, often lurking beneath the surface until they cause significant pain and impact quality of life. Unlike a limp or cloudy eyes, a cat with dental disease often hides its discomfort, making early detection and proactive care even more critical. This guide will walk you through the common dental issues affecting older cats, what you can do at home to prevent and manage them, and when it’s time to seek professional veterinary help. We’ll focus on practical steps and realistic expectations, so you can make informed decisions about your cat’s dental health.

Common Dental Problems in Senior Cats

As cats age, they become more susceptible to various dental issues. Understanding these problems is the first step in providing appropriate care.

Periodontal Disease

Periodontal disease is by far the most prevalent dental problem in senior cats. It’s an inflammatory condition affecting the tissues surrounding the teeth, including the gums, ligaments, and bone.

  • Plaque and Tartar Buildup: The process begins with plaque, a sticky film of bacteria that forms on teeth. If not removed, plaque hardens into tartar (calculus), which irritates the gums.
  • Gingivitis: The initial stage is gingivitis, characterized by red, swollen, and bleeding gums. At this stage, the damage is still reversible with professional cleaning and good home care.
  • Periodontitis: If gingivitis is left untreated, it progresses to periodontitis. The inflammation spreads deeper, destroying the tissues that support the teeth. This can lead to loose teeth, pain, and eventually tooth loss.
  • Systemic Effects: The bacteria involved in periodontal disease can enter the bloodstream and potentially affect other organs, such as the heart, kidneys, and liver.

Tooth Resorption

Tooth resorption is a unique and painful condition where the tooth structure is gradually broken down by the body’s own cells. It’s one of the most common causes of tooth loss in cats.

  • The Process: The body essentially starts to dissolve the tooth, often beginning at the gum line. The cause isn’t fully understood, but it’s believed to involve inflammation and immune system activity.
  • Signs: Tooth resorption can be difficult to detect in its early stages. As it progresses, you might notice:
    • Redness or swelling of the gums
    • A small hole or defect in the tooth
    • Increased drooling
    • Difficulty eating or a preference for soft food
    • Head shyness or reluctance to be touched around the mouth
  • Treatment: The primary treatment for tooth resorption is extraction of the affected tooth. In some cases, a crown amputation (removing the crown of the tooth and sealing the root) may be an option, but this is less common.

Stomatitis

Stomatitis is a severe inflammation of the mouth lining, including the gums, tongue, and cheeks. It’s a complex condition with multiple potential causes.

  • Possible Causes: While the exact cause isn’t always clear, stomatitis is often associated with:
    • Underlying viral infections (e.g., feline calicivirus, feline herpesvirus)
    • Immune system dysfunction
    • Periodontal disease
  • Symptoms: Cats with stomatitis experience significant pain and discomfort. Common symptoms include:
    • Severe inflammation and ulceration of the mouth
    • Excessive drooling, often with blood
    • Difficulty eating or refusing food altogether
    • Weight loss
    • Poor grooming
    • Bad breath
  • Treatment: Stomatitis treatment is often challenging and may involve a combination of approaches:
    • Pain management (essential for improving quality of life)
    • Antibiotics to control secondary bacterial infections
    • Corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants to reduce inflammation
    • Dental extractions (in many cases, removing all or most of the teeth is necessary to resolve the inflammation)

Other Dental Problems

While periodontal disease, tooth resorption, and stomatitis are the most common, senior cats can also experience other dental issues:

  • Fractured Teeth: Trauma or chewing on hard objects can lead to fractured teeth, which can be painful and prone to infection.
  • Oral Tumors: Oral cancer is more common in older cats. Tumors can cause swelling, pain, and difficulty eating.
  • Abscessed Teeth: An infection around the root of a tooth can lead to an abscess, causing swelling, pain, and drainage.

Prevention and Home Care for Senior Cat Dental Problems

While professional veterinary care is essential, there’s a lot you can do at home to prevent and manage dental problems in your senior cat.

Regular Tooth Brushing

Tooth brushing is the gold standard for preventing plaque and tartar buildup. However, it requires patience and consistency.

  • Start Slowly: If your cat isn’t used to having its teeth brushed, introduce the process gradually.
    1. Begin by simply touching your cat’s mouth and teeth with your finger.
    2. Once your cat is comfortable with this, introduce a pet-specific toothpaste. Never use human toothpaste, as it contains ingredients that are toxic to cats.
    3. Let your cat lick the toothpaste from your finger.
    4. Gradually start rubbing the toothpaste on your cat’s teeth with your finger.
    5. Finally, introduce a soft-bristled toothbrush designed for cats.
  • Brushing Technique:
    • Use a gentle, circular motion to brush the outer surfaces of the teeth.
    • Focus on the area where the gums meet the teeth.
    • Don’t worry about brushing the inner surfaces of the teeth, as cats are less prone to plaque buildup in these areas.
  • Frequency: Ideally, you should brush your cat’s teeth daily. However, even brushing a few times a week can make a significant difference.

Certain dental diets and treats can help reduce plaque and tartar buildup through mechanical action or chemical ingredients.

  • Dental Diets: These diets often have larger kibble sizes and a unique texture that helps scrape plaque off the teeth as the cat chews. Regular Veterinary Dental Checkups

Even with diligent home care, it’s crucial to schedule regular veterinary dental checkups. Your veterinarian can identify early signs of dental problems that you might miss at home.

  • Frequency: Most senior cats should have a dental checkup at least once a year, and possibly more frequently if they have a history of dental problems.
  • Professional cleaning: Your veterinarian may recommend a professional dental cleaning (prophylaxis) under anesthesia to remove plaque and tartar buildup, assess the health of the teeth and gums, and address any underlying dental issues.

Recognizing Signs of Dental Problems

Cats are masters at hiding pain, so it’s essential to be vigilant and watch for subtle signs of dental problems.

  • Changes in Eating Habits:
    • Decreased appetite or refusal to eat
    • Dropping food while eating
    • Chewing on one side of the mouth
    • Preference for soft food over dry food
    • Hesitation or reluctance to eat from a bowl
  • Behavioral Changes:
    • Increased irritability or aggression, especially when touched around the head or mouth
    • Reduced grooming
    • Hiding or withdrawing from social interaction
    • Excessive drooling
    • Pawing at the mouth
    • Head shaking
  • Physical Signs:
    • Bad breath (halitosis)
    • Red, swollen, or bleeding gums
    • Loose or missing teeth
    • Discolored teeth (yellow, brown, or black)
    • Visible tartar buildup
    • Swelling or lumps in the mouth or face

If you notice any of these signs, it’s essential to schedule a veterinary appointment as soon as possible.

Veterinary Treatment Options

Depending on the specific dental problem and its severity, your veterinarian may recommend various treatment options.

  • Professional Dental Cleaning (Prophylaxis): This involves scaling and polishing the teeth under anesthesia to remove plaque and tartar. Your veterinarian will also assess the health of each tooth and gum and take X-rays to evaluate the roots and bone.
  • Tooth Extraction: If a tooth is severely damaged, diseased, or causing pain, extraction may be necessary.
  • Root Canal Therapy: In some cases, root canal therapy may be an option to save a tooth. This involves removing the infected pulp from the tooth and filling the root canal.
  • Antibiotics: Antibiotics may be prescribed to treat bacterial infections associated with dental problems.
  • Pain Management: Pain medication is crucial for managing dental pain and improving your cat’s quality of life. Your veterinarian may prescribe non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or other pain relievers.
  • Surgery: In cases of oral tumors or severe stomatitis, surgery may be necessary to remove affected tissues.

Ultimately, proactive home care combined with regular veterinary dental checkups is the best way to keep your senior cat smiling – and comfortable – for years to come.
